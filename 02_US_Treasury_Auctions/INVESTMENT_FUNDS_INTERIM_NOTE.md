# Research Note — Who is behind “Investment Funds” in 30-Year Treasury Auctions?

**Status:** Interim research note  
**Date:** 2026-10-06

## Why this matters

Our 30-Year U.S. Treasury auction dataset shows that the Treasury investor-class category **Investment Funds** rose from roughly **53% of issuance in 2016 to about 63% in 2026 (through July in the current investor-class workbook)**, while Dealers & Brokers declined materially.

This does **not** mean that one homogeneous class of “investment funds” is financing the U.S. government.

Treasury’s investor-class definition groups together different actors, including:

- Mutual Funds
- Money Market Funds
- Hedge Funds
- Money Managers
- Investment Advisers

The economic owner of the money, the institution managing it, the entity bidding at auction, the holder of the cash Treasury security, and the holder of the interest-rate risk can therefore be different entities.

## Working map

### 1. Long-term / hedging demand

End investors such as:

- individuals
- pension funds
- insurance companies
- endowments
- institutional separate accounts

may delegate investment decisions to asset managers.

Asset managers can obtain long-duration Treasury exposure either by buying cash Treasuries or by using Treasury futures.

### 2. Hedge-fund / relative-value demand

Hedge funds can buy cash Treasuries for reasons unrelated to a desire to hold U.S. government debt for 30 years.

Examples include:

- cash-futures basis trades
- swap-spread arbitrage
- maturity-matched relative-value trades
- curve steepener / flattener trades

A typical cash-futures basis trade can involve:

1. buying the cash Treasury;
2. financing it in the repo market;
3. selling Treasury futures;
4. attempting to capture the small cash-futures price differential.

Thus a hedge fund may appear as a large holder/buyer of Treasury securities even though its economic objective is arbitrage rather than long-term lending to the U.S. government.

## The important distinction

The original question was:

> Who is lending money to the U.S. government for 30 years, and what yield must Treasury pay?

The data suggest that this needs to be decomposed into several questions:

1. **Who ultimately supplies the capital?**
2. **Who manages that capital?**
3. **Who bids in the Treasury auction?**
4. **Who holds the cash Treasury security?**
5. **Who ultimately bears the duration / interest-rate exposure?**
6. **Is the Treasury held as an investment, a hedge, or an arbitrage leg?**

These are not necessarily the same actor.

## Evidence found so far

- Treasury Investor Class data place a large share of 30-Year auction allotments in **Investment Funds**.
- Treasury’s Investment Funds category is heterogeneous and does not separately disclose mutual funds, hedge funds, money managers and investment advisers.
- Federal Reserve research documents very large hedge-fund Treasury exposures and decomposes important portions into basis trades, swap-spread arbitrage and other relative-value strategies.
- CFTC/TBAC analysis shows a structural relationship in Treasury futures in which **Asset Manager / Institutional** accounts tend to be strongly long while **Leveraged Funds** (principally hedge funds) tend to be strongly short.
- This can create a “symbiotic” structure: asset managers demand Treasury duration through futures, while hedge funds take the other side in futures and buy cash Treasuries financed through repo.

## Current hypothesis — not yet a conclusion

The rise of “Investment Funds” in 30-Year Treasury auctions may represent a mixture of:

**A. Asset-manager demand**  
Long-duration exposure for mutual funds, pensions, insurers, endowments and institutional mandates.

**B. Hedge-fund demand**  
Cash Treasury positions used as legs of leveraged relative-value / arbitrage strategies.

Therefore, the 63% Investment Funds figure cannot yet be interpreted as 63% of investors choosing to lend to the U.S. government for 30 years.

## Next test

Focus specifically on the 30-Year Treasury Bond / Ultra Bond complex and compare, over 2016–2026:

- Treasury 30-Year auction Investment Funds allotment
- CFTC Asset Manager / Institutional positioning
- CFTC Leveraged Funds positioning
- hedge-fund cash Treasury exposure where available
- repo financing / basis-trade evidence

The objective is to determine whether the changing auction buyer mix is associated primarily with long-duration asset-management demand, leveraged relative-value demand, or both.
